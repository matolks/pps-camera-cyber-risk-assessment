# Problem Statement

Nuclear power plants use network-connected digital systems to support Physical Protection System (PPS) functions. One example is a network-connected security camera used for alarm assessment, access verification, and situational awareness. These cameras improve operator visibility, but they also introduce cyber attack paths that were not present in older analog camera systems.

Under 10 CFR 73.54, nuclear licensees must protect digital computer and communication systems and networks that perform or support safety, security, and emergency preparedness (SSEP) functions. If compromise of a digital asset could adversely affect one of those functions, the asset may be treated as a Critical Digital Asset (CDA). For a PPS camera, the CDA decision depends on the camera's credited function, installation location, connectivity, and consequence of compromise. The decision is not based only on whether the device has a known vulnerability.

A practical issue in nuclear and operational technology (OT) environments is that vulnerable systems cannot always be patched immediately. Firmware or software updates may be delayed by vendor support limits, qualification requirements, system dependencies, or continuous availability requirements. As a result, cybersecurity work in these environments often depends on compensating controls in addition to patching.

This project evaluated the cyber risk of an unpatched, network-connected PPS camera in a simulated nuclear facility environment. The work included CDA determination, asset characterization, vulnerability analysis, attack pathway modeling, consequence analysis, and mitigation design. The final mitigation strategy used network segmentation, reverse proxy filtering, and firewall rules to reduce exploitability while preserving the camera's PPS function.
