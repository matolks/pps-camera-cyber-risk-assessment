## Problem Statement

Nuclear power plants use network-connected digital systems to support Physical Protection System (PPS) functions. One example is an IP-based security camera used for alarm assessment, access verification, and situational awareness. These cameras improve visibility, but they also create cyber attack paths that were not present in older analog systems.

Under 10 CFR 73.54, nuclear licensees must protect digital computer and communication systems that perform or support safety, security, and emergency preparedness (SSEP) functions. If compromising a digital asset could negatively affect one of those functions, the asset may be treated as a Critical Digital Asset (CDA). For a PPS camera, the CDA decision depends on what the camera supports, where it is installed, and what would happen if its video feed were lost or manipulated. It is not based only on whether the device has a known vulnerability.

A practical issue in nuclear and operational technology environments is that vulnerable systems cannot always be patched right away. Firmware or software updates may be delayed by vendor limits, qualification requirements, system dependencies, or the need to keep equipment continuously available. Because of that, cybersecurity work in these environments often depends on compensating controls instead of patching alone.

This project evaluated the cyber risk of an unpatched, network-connected PPS camera in a simulated nuclear facility environment. The work included CDA determination, asset characterization, vulnerability analysis, attack pathway modeling, consequence analysis, and mitigation design. The final mitigation strategy used network segmentation, reverse proxy filtering, and firewall rules to reduce exploitability while keeping the camera available for PPS use.
